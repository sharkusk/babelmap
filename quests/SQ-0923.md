--- note 2026-08-19T03:07:41Z ---

The hypothesis, and why the user's ear is evidence rather than preference: Sherlock plays three heartbeats (effects 11, 12, 13) off ONE sample against three pitch files reading 68, 74 and 79. SQ-0912 concluded the Amiga plays all three identically because every Amiga `.mid` is a unison pair — note == base within each file, so the ratio is 1. The user confirms that is what babelmap does, and that the Macintosh (which does bend them) sounds right while the Amiga does not.

What makes the unison reading suspect:

1. **`base` is 68 on ALL FOUR Macintosh index files** — M8, M11, M13, M14 — while `note` varies. Those four do not share a sample (M11 and M13 point at S12; M8 and M14 do not), so `base` cannot be a property of the sample, and it is constant across effects, so it is not a property of the effect either. A constant that survives both is a **per-game reference note**, not a per-file value.

2. The Amiga `.mid` files hold the same note TWICE — (68,68), (74,74), (79,79). If the interpreter takes `note` from the file and `base` from a per-game constant of 68, the Amiga bends by 0, +6 and +11 semitones — **exactly the Macintosh's model**, and the duplicated second byte in the `.mid` is padding rather than meaning.

3. Our own disassembly note (recorded on `Pitch` in `crates/blorb/src/infocom_sound.rs`) already says the routine at 0x48da in hunk 8 of the Amiga Lurking Horror takes "one note read from the pitch buffer at the cursor and one from a **per-effect field the interpreter captured earlier**". Whether that captured field is the pitch file's own second byte or something from elsewhere is the whole question, and it was assumed rather than traced.

4. It would explain the Blorb, which currently reads as incoherent: `stories/Sherlock.blb` fits `note − 74` and is "self-inconsistent on effect 13". If the reference is per-game — 74 for Lurking Horror, 68 for Sherlock — then the Blorb's author found 74 on Lurking Horror and carried it to Sherlock, where it is the wrong constant. That turns a third rendition from noise into corroboration.

What to do: re-read the Amiga interpreter around 0x48da and find where the second note comes from — story file, interpreter constant, or the pitch buffer. If it is not the file's own second byte, the Amiga model changes and `every_amiga_pitch_file_is_a_unison_pair` plus `the_macintosh_release_bends_the_shared_heartbeat` both need revisiting, along with the `Pitch` docs, which currently present the unison as a finding.

Do NOT settle this by ear alone — but do note that machine-screenshots/ and an emulator running the Amiga release are the falsifying instrument here, per the standing rule that internal measurement cannot tell you the question was wrong. A recording of Sherlock's three heartbeats on a real Amiga would end the argument in one pass.

--- note 2026-08-19T03:38:41Z ---

SETTLED, and the hypothesis in the opening note was wrong in an instructive way. `base` is not a per-game constant and it is not in the pitch file at all — **it is in the SAMPLE's header**. Read out of the 68000, not inferred.

## The disassembly

`The Lurking Horror` (42,224 bytes, off Lost Treasures disk 4) is a 147-hunk AmigaDOS load file; hunk 8 is CODE at file 0x4298..0x51cc. The three doubles the earlier note cited are immediates inside it, at hunk offsets 0x6a6 (1.05946309 = 2^(1/12)), 0x6d8 (1000.0) and 0x6ea (3579.49, the NTSC colour-burst clock in kHz). Method: `pip install capstone`, CS_ARCH_M68K/CS_MODE_BIG_ENDIAN, plus a hand-rolled hunk parser for the reloc tables (same-hunk calls are `bsr` and carry no relocation, which is why nothing appeared to reference the routine).

Pitch routine at hunk offset 0x642, ONE caller — the `bsr.w $642` at 0x4c2 inside the single big MIDI-event routine spanning 0x000..0x640. It computes

    ioa_Period = (3579.49 / (rate / 1000)) / 2^((noteA - noteB) / 12)

Period is inversely proportional to frequency, so the sound plays at `rate * 2^((noteA - noteB)/12)`.

The call site says what the notes are:

    0x4a0  movea.l $40.l, a0       ; the loaded pitch buffer
    0x4aa  move.b  (a0), d2        ;   noteA = pitchbuf[cursor + 1]
    0x4ac  movea.l $c(a7), a0
    0x4b0  adda.l  #$78, a0        ; a per-channel byte array
    0x4b8  move.b  (a0), d3        ;   noteB = bases[channel]

and `bases[channel]` was loaded at 0x41a from `desc[1]` — byte 1 of the loaded sample. The loader strips the two-byte length field, so desc[1] is **file offset 3: the low byte of the header's flags word**.

The same routine also settles the rest of that header: `desc[0]` (flags high byte) -> `ioa_Cycles` at 0x3aa; `desc + 8` -> `ioa_Data` at 0x396; the word at `desc + 6` (frame count) -> `ioa_Length` at 0x3c8. So `flags` = repeat count in the high byte, base note in the low. It was documented as "not yet understood" for two quests.

And the note is transposed first: at 0x43c/0x45c the routine subtracts 0x18 from `pitchbuf[cursor+1]` when the status byte is 0x90 or 0x91 and 0x0c when it is 0x92 — two octaves down for MIDI channels 0 and 1, one for channel 2 — writing it back in place. Every file of both games is channel 0, so the effective rule is `note - 24 - base`.

## Why the old reading was wrong

The eleven bytes are `00 09 | 9c note 40 | FF 00 nn | 9c note 00` — a Note-On and its matching **Note-Off**. SQ-0912 read the second note as a reference pitch, saw it equal the first on all 22 Amiga files, and concluded the Amiga never bends. It equals the first because that is what a Note-Off does.

The Macintosh M-files looked like the pair "coming apart" only because whoever made them copied `heart1.mid` and edited the Note-ON: M11's first 11 bytes are byte-identical to Sherlock's `heart1.mid`, and M8/M13/M14 differ from their Amiga counterparts in the Note-OFF byte alone, all still reading 68. A stale byte in four files, read as a discovery.

## Corpus verification: 27 of 29

With `(note - transposition) - base_note`, the decoded rate matches `stories/Lurking.blb` and `stories/Sherlock.blb` EXACTLY on 27 of the 29 effects they carry — all 18 the model leaves unbent and all 9 it bends. Sherlock's `heart` gives 13032 / 18430 / 24601 Hz for effects 11 / 12 / 13, and 13032 is exactly what the Blorb states for effect 11. Effect 8 -> 9123 and effect 14 -> 16419, both exact.

The unison reading agreed on the 18 and disagreed on all 9 — the shape of a model that is only ever right when the answer is zero.

The two misses are the two known anomalies: Lurking Horror 17 (stated 32910 Hz already needs a Paula period of 109, past the hardware minimum of 124, before any bend) and Sherlock 13 (the Blorb's entry is a differently-trimmed take, 13,989 frames against its siblings' 13,999, and its rate matches note 72 where the disk says 79 — two independent signs of one botched entry).

Also incidentally explained: the Blorb author's `note - 74` rule. For a status-0x90 file against base 50, `note - 24 - 50` IS `note - 74`. They had the right answer for Sherlock and a different base (60) for most of Lurking Horror, which is why `note - 74` looked self-inconsistent across the two games.

## The one thing that is ours, not Infocom's

The routine writes its result into `ioa_Period` with no bounds check (0x4ce) — the hardware is what refuses to go faster. `PAULA_CEILING_HZ = 3_579_490 / 124` caps the effective rate, using the interpreter's own clock constant and the AHRM's documented minimum DMA period. It bites on exactly one sound in either game, Lurking Horror 17, which without it would play at 82,928 Hz. **This clamp is a judgement call and is flagged as such in the source** — the minimum-period figure is the only number here not read out of the binary.

## Method note

The decisive evidence was cheap and should be reached for sooner: capstone disassembles m68k out of the box, and a 40-line AmigaDOS hunk parser turns a 147-hunk load file into addressable code. Two quests of inference over the corpus (SQ-0907, SQ-0912) were settled in one pass by reading the program. Where a shipped interpreter for the platform exists, disassemble it before modelling its behaviour from its data files.
